I wrote a piece for Torchbox this week about a blind spot in most usability testing: [what happens when people never reach your website at all](https://torchbox.com/news/when-ai-becomes-the-front-door-to-your-website/).

## The £13 answer

It starts with someone trying to register a charity. They ask an AI summary how much it costs, and get told £13. The real figure is £100. The AI had pulled the number from an old, unmaintained page on a government site, one that hadn't been updated in years and that almost nobody visits directly any more. The person never saw the original page. They just saw the answer, and believed it.

## Why usability testing misses this

Most usability testing still assumes someone lands on your site and clicks through it. That assumption is ageing badly. Around 69.5% of UK Google searches now end without a single click to any website. When an AI summary appears above the results, click-through drops further still, from 15% down to 8%.

People are getting their answer from a layer that sits between them and your content. If that layer gets something wrong, your usability testing will never catch it, because the person you're testing never went near the page in question.

## The ghost pages problem

The pages most at risk are the ones nobody thinks about. GOV.UK alone has around 700,000 published pages. Charities keep old financial records live for transparency, and donors ask AI tools about them anyway. None of these pages get much traffic through normal search. All of them are fair game for an AI system to crawl, summarise and potentially misquote.

Nobody is testing what AI says about these pages, because nobody thought of them as user-facing in the first place.

## What organisations need to do differently

The response isn't to abandon usability testing. It's to add a layer most research plans don't have yet: checking what AI actually says about your organisation, mapping the parts of your content estate that AI might surface, and monitoring that over time rather than treating it as a one-off audit.

This matters more than it might sound. After Air Canada's chatbot gave a customer wrong information about bereavement fares, a tribunal held the airline responsible for what its AI had said. Organisations are already accountable for these answers. Most just haven't started checking what those answers are.
